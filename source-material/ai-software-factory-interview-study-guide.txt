Chapter 2: The Problem You Are Solving

A strong interview answer begins with the problem, not the solution.

The current engineering model

Most software organizations still rely on a largely sequential operating model:

Humans analyze → humans plan → humans implement → humans test → humans review → humans deploy → humans monitor → humans investigate → humans document.

This model contains large amounts of delay.

A developer may spend only a few hours making a change, while the work takes several days to move through the organization.

The delay comes from:

* Waiting for requirements clarification.
* Waiting for technical investigation.
* Waiting for implementation.
* Waiting for test environments.
* Waiting for test execution.
* Waiting for reviews.
* Waiting for approvals.
* Waiting for deployment windows.
* Waiting for incident analysis.
* Waiting for documentation.

The largest opportunity is often not faster typing.

It is reducing waiting and coordination.

The limitations of current AI adoption

Many organizations are adding AI tools without redesigning the operating model.

They give developers coding assistants, but the overall process remains largely unchanged.

This can create:

* More code without more value.
* Faster implementation but slower review.
* Inconsistent agent behavior.
* Weak accountability.
* Unclear permissions.
* Limited auditability.
* Increased security risk.
* More generated defects.
* New review bottlenecks.
* Difficulty measuring business impact.

Your point of view should be:

Coding agents improve individual tasks. An AI Software Factory redesigns the entire flow of engineering work.

The executive problem statement

Engineering organizations need a way to increase delivery speed and leverage without increasing operational risk, coordination complexity, and headcount at the same rate.

The developer problem statement

Engineers spend too much time performing repetitive execution and waiting for systems or people instead of solving difficult customer and architectural problems.

The quality problem statement

Organizations cannot safely increase autonomy unless they can continuously prove that agent-generated changes satisfy functional, security, performance, reliability, and policy requirements.

The leadership problem statement

Leaders lack a control plane that shows what agents are doing, why they are doing it, what they are allowed to access, what evidence they produced, what the work cost, and where human approval is required.

This is why Mission Control matters.

Chapter 5: Mission Control

The simplest explanation

Mission Control is the governance and orchestration layer for the AI Software Factory.

A coding agent performs a task.

Mission Control determines:

* What work should happen.
* Why it should happen.
* Which agent should perform it.
* What tools the agent may use.
* What constraints apply.
* What evidence must be produced.
* Which human must approve.
* What the work costs.
* Whether the outcome is acceptable.
* What happened after deployment.
* What the system should learn.

Why “control plane” is useful terminology

In technology, a control plane coordinates and governs the behavior of underlying systems.

Mission Control does not need to perform every engineering task itself.

It coordinates specialized agents and systems that perform the work.

You can explain it this way:

Coding agents are the workers. Mission Control is the operating system that assigns work, applies policy, coordinates execution, captures evidence, routes approvals, and measures outcomes.

Layer 3: Execution

Specialized agents perform:

Chapter 6: Essential Terminology

You should be able to define these terms naturally.

AI agent

An AI agent is a system that can interpret a goal, reason about the work, use tools, take actions, observe results, and continue until it reaches a completion or escalation condition.

Autonomous agent

An autonomous agent can perform multiple steps without requiring human instruction at every stage, but it still operates within permissions, policies, budgets, and escalation boundaries.

Agentic workflow

An agentic workflow is a structured sequence in which one or more agents reason, use tools, make decisions, and complete work toward an objective.

Multi-agent system

A multi-agent system uses specialized agents with different roles, such as investigation, planning, implementation, testing, security review, and deployment validation.

Orchestration

Orchestration coordinates which agent performs which task, in what order, with what context, permissions, dependencies, and completion conditions.

Mission

A mission is a governed unit of business or engineering intent with an objective, owner, constraints, risk level, lifecycle, and measurable completion criteria.

Work order

A work order is a structured assignment given to an agent or team. It defines the task, allowed tools, inputs, expected outputs, evidence requirements, and operational limits.

Tool use

Tool use allows agents to interact with external systems such as GitHub, Jira, CI/CD, databases, cloud environments, testing tools, and observability platforms.

MCP

Model Context Protocol is a standard approach for exposing tools and contextual resources to AI systems through defined interfaces.

For an interview, do not become trapped in protocol details.

Say:

MCP provides a standardized way for agents to discover and use enterprise tools and context.

Context engineering

Context engineering is the discipline of supplying an agent with the right information, instructions, tools, history, policies, and constraints at the right time.

Memory

Memory allows a system to preserve useful information across steps or sessions. Memory may include mission state, past decisions, user preferences, system knowledge, or previous outcomes.

Retrieval-augmented generation

RAG combines model reasoning with retrieved enterprise knowledge, such as documentation, tickets, code, and operational records.

Human in the loop

Human in the loop means a person reviews, approves, corrects, or guides work at defined points.

Human on the loop

Human on the loop means the system operates autonomously within policy while a person supervises performance and handles exceptions.

Human out of the loop

Human out of the loop means a workflow can complete without human intervention. This is appropriate only for sufficiently understood, low-risk, and well-controlled work.

Evaluation

An evaluation measures whether an agent or workflow performed correctly, reliably, safely, and usefully.

Deterministic validation

Deterministic validation produces repeatable outcomes based on explicit rules, such as test results, schema validation, security policies, or build success.

Model-based evaluation

Model-based evaluation uses an AI model to assess qualities that may be difficult to measure with deterministic rules, such as plan completeness or explanation quality.

Evidence

Evidence is the verifiable output supporting a claim of completion. Examples include passing tests, deployment logs, security results, diffs, screenshots, and telemetry.

Observability

Observability provides visibility into agent actions, decisions, tool calls, failures, latency, cost, and outcomes.

Guardrail

A guardrail is a control that limits or redirects agent behavior. It may include permissions, policies, validations, budgets, or execution boundaries.

Policy engine

A policy engine evaluates rules and determines whether an action may proceed, requires approval, or must be blocked.

Sandboxing

Sandboxing isolates agent execution to prevent unintended access or damage.

Model routing

Model routing selects the most appropriate model based on task type, complexity, cost, latency, risk, or confidentiality.

Progressive autonomy

Progressive autonomy means agents earn greater authority as their reliability is demonstrated.

Continuous validation

Continuous validation means verifying assumptions, plans, changes, deployments, and production behavior throughout the lifecycle rather than testing only before release.

AI Software Factory

An AI Software Factory is the complete technical and organizational system that converts human intent into validated software outcomes through governed collaboration between humans and autonomous agents.

Chapter 7: Risk-Based Autonomy

The safest and most credible vision does not assume every agent should have full autonomy.

Level 0: Advisory

The agent researches and recommends.

It cannot make changes.

Example:

An agent analyzes an incident and proposes likely causes.

Level 1: Drafting

The agent produces an artifact, but a human executes or submits it.

Example:

The agent writes an implementation plan or code patch for review.

Level 2: Supervised execution

The agent executes in a controlled environment, but a human approves completion.

Example:

The agent changes code and creates a pull request, but cannot merge.

Level 3: Governed autonomy

The agent completes approved categories of low- or medium-risk work within policy.

Example:

The agent updates documentation, fixes low-risk dependencies, or adds tests.

Level 4: Continuous autonomy

The agent detects, repairs, validates, and deploys approved classes of changes.

Example:

The agent identifies a broken test, repairs it, validates the change, and deploys within established controls.

Level 5: Factory autonomy

Multiple agents coordinate across the lifecycle, while humans govern objectives, policies, risk, exceptions, and strategic decisions.

The interview principle

Autonomy should be granted by workflow and risk class, not by giving an agent universal authority.

A strong example

A documentation correction may be allowed to proceed autonomously. A standard application change may require human pull-request approval. A security-sensitive production change may require architecture, security, and release approval. The level of human control should match the potential impact.

The workflow:

1. A human submits an issue.
2. The system clarifies the objective and acceptance criteria.
3. An investigation agent analyzes the relevant code and architecture.
4. A planning agent creates an implementation and test plan.
5. A human approves the plan based on risk.
6. An implementation agent modifies the code.
7. A testing agent creates and runs tests.

Chapter 16: Interview Questions You Should Expect

“Are you trying to replace developers?”

No. I am trying to change where developers spend their attention. Engineers should spend less time on repetitive investigation, mechanical implementation, manual validation, and coordination. They should spend more time understanding customers, shaping architecture, evaluating tradeoffs, solving novel problems, and reviewing consequential decisions. The model changes the work more than it eliminates the need for human judgment.

“Why not just use a coding assistant?”

Coding assistants improve an individual task. The larger opportunity is to redesign the complete engineering workflow. Companies need coordination across planning, implementation, testing, security, deployment, operations, evidence, and approval. That requires an operating model and control plane, not only a code-generation tool.

“What prevents agents from causing damage?”

Risk-based permissions, isolated execution, minimum-required access, deterministic validation, policy enforcement, spending and execution limits, human approval for consequential actions, complete audit trails, and progressive autonomy. Agents should earn authority based on demonstrated reliability within a specific workflow.

“How do you measure whether it works?”

I would measure speed, quality, economics, human impact, and agent reliability. Examples include lead time, plan-to-PR time, change failure rate, escaped defects, human hours per work item, agent cost, rework, developer satisfaction, intervention rate, and first-pass validation. The goal is measurable customer and engineering value, not code volume.

“What would you build first?”

A governed issue-to-pull-request workflow for clearly scoped, low-risk changes. It is measurable, understandable, useful across engineering teams, and exercises the core capabilities: investigation, planning, approval, implementation, testing, review, evidence, and production validation.

“What remains human?”

Customer understanding, strategic direction, architecture, ethical judgment, risk tolerance, ambiguous tradeoffs, team leadership, high-impact approvals, accountability, and exception handling. Agents can support those areas, but responsibility remains human.

“How do you avoid creating more review work?”

The system should produce structured evidence rather than simply larger volumes of generated code. Work should be decomposed into smaller changes, validated automatically, summarized clearly, and routed according to risk. Review burden is itself a metric. If agent output increases human review time, the workflow is not yet delivering leverage.

“What is the biggest risk?”

Deploying autonomy faster than the organization’s ability to evaluate and govern it. Agent capability may advance faster than trust, controls, and operational readiness. That is why I favor progressive autonomy, evidence-based advancement, and workflow-specific permissions.

“How would the engineering organization change?”

Engineers become more focused on intent, architecture, judgment, and review. Managers shift from coordinating task execution toward designing systems, managing risk, developing talent, and improving decision quality. Quality moves from a downstream testing function to a continuous validation capability embedded throughout the lifecycle.

Chapter 21: How to Sound More Executive

AI can reduce repetitive engineering execution, but the business value depends on whether the complete workflow becomes faster without increasing defects, review burden, or operational risk.

Instead of:

We want agents to do everything.

Say:

We want to assign autonomy by workflow and risk level, expanding authority only when reliability is demonstrated.

Instead of:

The agent checks the work.

The validation layer produces deterministic and model-based evidence that the work satisfies acceptance, security, quality, and policy requirements.

Instead of:

Humans are still involved.

Humans retain ownership of intent, consequential decisions, risk acceptance, and final accountability.

Instead of:

This produces more code.

This reduces the time from intent to validated customer value.

Chapter 25: Common Mistakes to Avoid

Do not describe agents as magical.

Do not imply that model intelligence eliminates the need for engineering discipline.

Do not equate code generation with productivity.

Do not claim full autonomy is the starting point.

Do not ignore security, cost, or review burden.

Do not describe humans as passive approvers.

Do not present one general-purpose agent as the entire architecture.

Do not use terminology you cannot explain simply.

Do not speak only about technology.

Do not forget customers, economics, organization, and change management.

Do not overstate what is already proven.

Use language such as:

* “My thesis is…”
* “The model I would test is…”
* “The evidence I would require is…”
* “I would begin with…”
* “I would expand only after…”

That communicates confidence without pretending uncertainty does not exist.

Chapter 27: The Final Study Script

Read this aloud every day until it becomes natural.

Software engineering is moving from a human-operated model to a human-led, AI-executed model.

My vision is to create and lead an AI Software Factory where humans define intent, make consequential decisions, and remain accountable, while autonomous agents perform increasing amounts of investigation, planning, implementation, testing, validation, documentation, and operational execution.

The purpose is not to remove engineers. It is to remove repetitive labor, unnecessary waiting, coordination overhead, and manual validation so people can focus on customer understanding, architecture, creativity, judgment, and leadership.

The factory requires more than capable agents. It needs an operating model and a control plane. Mission Control determines what work should happen, which agent should perform it, what tools it may use, what evidence is required, which human must approve, what the work costs, and what happened after deployment.

I believe autonomy must be earned. Low-risk workflows can operate with limited intervention, while higher-risk changes require deeper human review. Every action should be traceable, and every material outcome should be supported by evidence.

Quality is the acceleration engine. Reliable validation creates trust, trust allows greater autonomy, and greater autonomy reduces cycle time. The goal is not more code. The goal is faster delivery of validated customer value with better quality, economics, traceability, and control.

I would begin with a governed issue-to-pull-request workflow, establish baseline metrics, prove improved speed without increased defects, and then expand into test maintenance, defect remediation, security, incidents, production validation, and operations.

My background in engineering leadership, quality, automation, enterprise platforms, and AI gives me a strong foundation to lead this transformation. I understand both the technical system and the human organization that must operate around it.

My long-term mission is to demonstrate that engineering organizations can move dramatically faster without becoming reckless, automate aggressively without abandoning human responsibility, and use AI to improve productivity and quality at the same time.
